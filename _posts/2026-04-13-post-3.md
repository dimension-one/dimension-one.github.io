This post closes the first group of posts built around the same 1d classification example.
In that example, post 1 showed that useful explanations of generalization cannot ignore the data, and post 2 identified the exact quantity that remains at interpolation:
Bayes error plus a disagreement term with the Bayes classifier.

That raises a natural question:
> do popular complexity measures for generalization say something about the mass of the region where the classifier has the wrong sign, or are they really measuring something else?

All the complexity measures we look at do depend on the learned decision rule, so they are eligible candidates to explain generalization in this setup.
In particular, they do not fall in the “data-agnostic” category of post 1. 
But being an eligible candidate is not the same as tracking the right object. 

We stay in the same setup as before:

- {::nomarkdown}$X\sim \mathrm{Unif}([-1,1])${:/nomarkdown}
- label {::nomarkdown}$y(x)=\operatorname{sign}(x)${:/nomarkdown}
- with probability {::nomarkdown}$p${:/nomarkdown}, the label is replaced by a random sign {::nomarkdown}$\text{Unif}(\{-1,+1\})${:/nomarkdown}.

We train a 1-hidden-layer ReLU net for binary classification. 
Write its 2d output as {::nomarkdown}$f(x)=(f_1(x),f_2(x))${:/nomarkdown}.
Define the logit gap

{::nomarkdown}
$$
\Delta(x)=f_2(x)-f_1(x),
$$
{:/nomarkdown}

with predicted label


{::nomarkdown}
$$
\hat y(x) = \operatorname{sign}(\Delta(x)).
$$
{:/nomarkdown}


The population error is (proved in the previous post):

{::nomarkdown}
$$
\boxed{
\text{population error}(p)
=
p/2
+
(1-p)\,\mathrm{Leb\_wrong}/2
}
$$
{:/nomarkdown}

with the Lebesgue measure of the disagreement set: {::nomarkdown}$\mathrm{Leb\_wrong} = \mathrm{Leb}\{x\in[-1,1]: \operatorname{sign}(\Delta(x))\neq \operatorname{sign}(x)\}${:/nomarkdown}.

This formula is the takeaway of post 2. 
Once training error is zero, the classifier-dependent part of generalization is controlled by the disagreement region:
how much of the interval is predicted with the wrong sign.
That is the quantity the proxies should somehow capture. 

In more general classification settings, there is no similar easy formula since the Bayes classifier is not tractable. 
Instead, one falls back on proxies computed from the learned parameters:
margins, counts of decision changes, slopes, sharpness, and related quantities.

But in our 1d example, these indirect explanations can be compared directly to the exact Bayes-disagreement formula above.

> what do these proxies actually measure, compared with the quantity that really determines generalization here?

---

## usual proxies and their computation

Since we consider a one-hidden-layer ReLU net, 
many complexity measures are easy to compute. 
Indeed, each ReLU hidden unit {::nomarkdown}$x\mapsto \max(0,w x+b)${:/nomarkdown} is piecewise affine with a single breakpoint at

{::nomarkdown}
$$
x=-b/w.
$$
{:/nomarkdown}

If we sort these breakpoints, we get a partition of the domain into intervals on which {::nomarkdown}$\Delta${:/nomarkdown} is affine:

{::nomarkdown}
$$
\Delta(x)=m x + c.
$$
{:/nomarkdown}

Computing all these intervals and the corresponding slopes {::nomarkdown}$m${:/nomarkdown} and intercepts {::nomarkdown}$c${:/nomarkdown} is easy, and allows to compute many proxies exactly, such as:
- the number of affine pieces,
- the number of sign changes of {::nomarkdown}$\Delta${:/nomarkdown},
- slopes {::nomarkdown}$\lvert m \rvert${:/nomarkdown} on each region, in particular, the exact Lipschitz constant {::nomarkdown}$\max \lvert \Delta'(x) \rvert${:/nomarkdown}, 
or the averaged Lipschitz constant {::nomarkdown}$\int_{-1}^1 \lvert\Delta'(x)\rvert\,dx / 2${:/nomarkdown},
- margin-band mass {::nomarkdown}$\mathrm{Leb}\{\lvert\Delta(x)\rvert\le \gamma\}${:/nomarkdown},
- the same slopes and margin bands after dividing {::nomarkdown}$\Delta${:/nomarkdown} by its root-mean-square over {::nomarkdown}$[-1,1]${:/nomarkdown}, which removes the overall scale of the logits, and the corresponding normalized training margins,
- sharpness around the learned parameters,
- and of course {::nomarkdown}$\mathrm{Leb\_wrong}${:/nomarkdown}.

---

## two families of proxies

In this setup, most of the proxies we look at fall into one of two broad families.

### family A: decision-geometry proxies (about correctness of the decision)

These try to say something meaningful about the geometry of the disagreement set {::nomarkdown}$\{x\in[-1,1]: \operatorname{sign}(\Delta(x))\neq \operatorname{sign}(x)\}${:/nomarkdown}.

- **{::nomarkdown}$\mathrm{Leb\_wrong}${:/nomarkdown} itself.**  
  Not a proxy: it is the measure of the disagreement set, and yields the exact population error (up to the factor {::nomarkdown}$(1-p)/2${:/nomarkdown}), 
  and thus also the generalization gap if the training error is zero.

- **number of sign flips (roots) of {::nomarkdown}$\Delta${:/nomarkdown}.**  
  A coarse summary of how often the classifier changes its mind on {::nomarkdown}$[-1,1]${:/nomarkdown}.
  This is not a standard generalization proxy in modern settings, but here it is a natural candidate to track the level of corruption {::nomarkdown}$p${:/nomarkdown} at interpolation, since as {::nomarkdown}$p${:/nomarkdown} grows, the learned rule needs to flip its decision more and more often to fit the corrupted labels. 
  However, there is no direct link with {::nomarkdown}$\mathrm{Leb\_wrong}${:/nomarkdown}: 
  you can flip a lot while being wrong on a tiny set, or flip rarely while being wrong on a large set.

- **margin-band mass**
  {::nomarkdown}
  $$
  \mathrm{Leb}\{x\in[-1,1]: |\Delta(x)|\le \gamma\}.
  $$
  {:/nomarkdown}
  This measures how much input mass lives near the decision boundary {::nomarkdown}$\Delta=0${:/nomarkdown}.
  It says “how fragile are predictions under small perturbations in score”.
  It does *not* say “is the boundary placed correctly”. 
  In particular, 
  a classifier can be confidently wrong (large {::nomarkdown}$|\Delta|${:/nomarkdown} with wrong sign), 
  so band mass can be small while {::nomarkdown}$\mathrm{Leb\_wrong}${:/nomarkdown} is large. 
  So here, margins are mostly about robustness of the decision, not correctness.

### family B: scale / landscape proxies (about sensitivity of the decision, not correctness)

These are about “how wild” {::nomarkdown}$\Delta${:/nomarkdown} is, or how sensitive the parameters are.

- **slope / Lipschitz-type quantities in input space.**  
  Like the maximal slope {::nomarkdown}$\max \lvert \Delta'(x) \rvert${:/nomarkdown} over all {::nomarkdown}$x${:/nomarkdown}, 
  or the average slope {::nomarkdown}$\int_{-1}^1 \lvert\Delta'(x)\rvert\,dx / 2${:/nomarkdown}. 
  Large slopes mean large sensitivity to input perturbations.
  However, sensitivity has no direct link with whether the decision is correct (there are classifiers with large slope but small disagreement mass, and vice versa). 

  Note that while we will report exact versions of these slopes, in more general settings ones often replaces these exact slopes by upper bounds (the exact Lipschitz constant is NP-hard to compute in high dimension).

- **flatness / sharpness in parameter space.**  
  These measure how sensitive the training loss is to perturbations of the parameters.
  Sharpness can track memorization difficulty rather than disagreement geometry. 
  Here, interpolating increasingly corrupted labels requires more and more oscillations of the learned rule, and small parameter perturbations can easily break these oscillations and cause large loss increase, even if the disagreement mass (hence generalization gap) remains small. 

  These proxies are the only ones we consider that do depend on the parameters chosen to represent the learned decision rule. 
  The ReLU network used for classification admits several parameter values that represent the same function. Among these parameters, some can be sharper than others. 
  Below, we will report different notions of sharpness, each with different sensitivity to these symmetries.

---

## which proxies are closest to the right geometry?

A complexity measure should help choose among models trained on the same data:
given one training set, which of the networks that fit it will generalize better?
So we fix the corruption level at {::nomarkdown}$p=0.4${:/nomarkdown}, fix a training set, train several networks on it, and ask whether a proxy orders them like their generalization gaps.

For networks that interpolate the training set, the formula above becomes

{::nomarkdown}
$$
\mathrm{gap}=0.2+0.3\,\mathrm{Leb\_wrong},
$$
{:/nomarkdown}

so {::nomarkdown}$\mathrm{Leb\_wrong}${:/nomarkdown} orders them perfectly. It is the reference, not a competitor.

Before looking at the numbers, we can already expect a rough hierarchy in this 1d setup:

- {::nomarkdown}$\mathrm{Leb\_wrong}${:/nomarkdown} is exact, by the formula above.
- The sign-flip count is constrained by the training set: to interpolate, the learned rule must change sign at least once between consecutive training inputs with different labels. If the networks add no extra flips, the count is the same for all networks fitted to the same data, and cannot order them.
- Then should come sensitivity proxies such as margin-band mass, slopes, and sharpness, which are not directly about where the decision boundaries are placed.

## results

We draw 20 training sets of 32 points at {::nomarkdown}$p=0.4${:/nomarkdown}.
On each, we train 16 one-hidden-layer ReLU networks with Adam:
widths 256 and 1024, initialization scales 0.5 and 2 (multiplying the default initialization), learning rates 0.003 and 0.01, and two random initializations.
Training stops at zero training error and cross-entropy at most 0.02, within 200,000 steps.

232 of the 320 networks fit every training label.
We keep the training sets with at least 8 such networks: 15 sets, 206 networks.
For each training set, we compute the Spearman rank correlation between a proxy and the generalization gap, then average over the 15 training sets.
The intervals are 95% bootstrap intervals obtained by resampling training sets, not individual networks.

Proxies are sorted by the absolute value of their mean correlation:

| proxy | mean rank correlation with the gap | 95% interval |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| `Leb_wrong` (reference) | +1.00 | |
| `band_mass_γ`, all four {::nomarkdown}$\gamma\in\{0.1,0.2,0.5,1\}${:/nomarkdown} | −0.37 to −0.38 | [−0.52, −0.22] |
| `Lavg_flip` | +0.24 | [+0.06, +0.42] |
| normalized `band_mass_0.1` | −0.19 | [−0.40, +0.03] |
| normalized `band_mass_0.2` | −0.18 | [−0.37, +0.02] |
| normalized `Lavg` | +0.18 | [−0.01, +0.36] |
| `flat_rel` | −0.14 | [−0.35, +0.08] |
| normalized `band_mass_0.5` | −0.13 | [−0.35, +0.10] |
| `hess_top` | +0.09 | [−0.07, +0.26] |
| `Lavg` | +0.07 | [−0.18, +0.31] |
| `Lmax` | +0.07 | [−0.17, +0.28] |
| normalized `Lmax` | +0.03 | [−0.15, +0.19] |
| normalized `band_mass_1.0` | +0.02 | [−0.17, +0.21] |
| normalized margin, 10th percentile | +0.02 | [−0.23, +0.26] |
| `nb_regions` | −0.02 | [−0.16, +0.12] |
| normalized margin, minimum | +0.01 | [−0.22, +0.25] |
| `sam_sharp` | −0.01 | [−0.14, +0.13] |
| `nb_flips` | constant in 13 of the 15 training sets | |

The proxies are those listed above:
`nb_flips` counts sign changes of {::nomarkdown}$\Delta${:/nomarkdown};
`nb_regions` counts affine pieces;
`Lmax` and `Lavg` are the maximum and average of {::nomarkdown}$\lvert\Delta'(x)\rvert${:/nomarkdown}, and `Lavg_flip` the average over the affine pieces containing a root of {::nomarkdown}$\Delta${:/nomarkdown};
`band_mass_γ` is {::nomarkdown}$\mathrm{Leb}\{\lvert\Delta(x)\rvert\le\gamma\}${:/nomarkdown};
normalized versions use {::nomarkdown}$\Delta${:/nomarkdown} divided by its root-mean-square over {::nomarkdown}$[-1,1]${:/nomarkdown};
`flat_rel` is the mean training-loss increase under small relative Gaussian perturbations of the parameters, `sam_sharp` the increase after one normalized gradient-ascent step of radius {::nomarkdown}$10^{-3}${:/nomarkdown}, and `hess_top` a power-iteration estimate of the top Hessian eigenvalue.

**The decision-flip count cannot order the networks.**
In almost every case, the networks change sign exactly once per label change along the sorted training inputs, and no more.
The count is then a property of the training set, identical for all networks fitted to it.

**The next picture shows what the count misses.**
Both networks fit the same 32 points with 11 decision boundaries, yet their gaps are 35.3% and 39.7%.
The wrong-sign intervals (shaded) have different lengths because the boundaries sit at different places between training points.

![Two networks fitted to the same 32 labeled points, both with 11 decision boundaries, but different lengths of wrong-sign intervals.]({{ '/assets/images/post-3/same-data-boundaries.png' | relative_url }})

*The pair with the largest gap difference among networks with the same flip count on the same training set, selected for contrast. Each curve is divided by its own root-mean-square, which changes its vertical scale but not its boundaries.*

**Margin bands and boundary slopes show modest associations, of different natures.**
Raw margin-band mass measures how much of the input interval lies close to a decision boundary, in units of the logits.
Its association is the largest, and it is negative: more input mass near the boundary goes with a *smaller* gap, the opposite of the usual intuition that larger margins generalize better.
It weakens once the logits are divided by their root-mean-square.
The average slope on the affine pieces where {::nomarkdown}$\Delta${:/nomarkdown} crosses zero measures something else, how steeply the decision changes, and has a positive association.
Neither describes where the boundaries are placed relative to the clean rule, which is what determines the gap.
With correlations below 0.4 in absolute value, many quantities tested and only 15 training sets, these associations do not single out a reliable proxy.

**Sharpness, global slopes and the number of pieces show no clear association**: their intervals contain zero.

The experiment covers one architecture, one noise level and a small set of hyperparameters, and only the networks that fit every training label; wider networks and larger initializations reached zero training error more often.

These conclusions concern models fitted to a fixed training set.
Pooling the 232 interpolating networks across all 20 training sets answers a different question.
There, the flip count has rank correlation +0.51 with the gap, as much as the number of label changes in the training set, and the error of the nearest-neighbour rule on each training set reaches +0.96: across training sets, most of the variation in the gap comes from the training set itself.

## takeaway


This closes the first group of posts built around understanding generalization in the same 1d classification example.

In that example, we now know the target exactly:
at interpolation, the generalization gap equals the population error, namely Bayes error plus a term controlled by the mass of the region where the learned classifier disagrees with the Bayes rule.
Most common proxies do not measure that quantity. 
They measure how sensitive the learned rule is to change in inputs (slopes, margins), parameters (sharpness), or how often it changes prediction. 

The experiment illustrates that.
Among networks fitted to the same training set, only the exact geometric target orders them reliably.
The decision-flip count is fixed by the training labels.
The more generic proxies show weak associations at best: margin bands and slopes capture different geometric properties of the learned rule, but none of them is the geometry that determines generalization here, namely where the boundaries sit relative to the clean rule.

This is not specific to this 1d example: a fixed proxy only measures one aspect of the learned rule, and that aspect can be irrelevant to many Bayes-disagreement geometries that can arise in more general classification problems.
